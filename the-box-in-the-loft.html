A Note to the Reader

This is not an ordinary story. Nobody is going to tell you what happened.

Instead, you are going to see the evidence — the actual text messages, diary pages, newspaper cuttings and letters, in the order the sisters found them. You will know exactly what Flo and Delilah knew, at exactly the moment they knew it.

Which means you have precisely the same chance they had of solving the mystery first.

Keep your eyes open. Nothing in this box is here by accident.

MONDAY 24 AUGUST 1987PRICE 22p
MYSTERY WRITER VANISHES
FROM SEAGULL HOTEL
Queen of the seaside murder mystery missing from Room 9 — cardigan found at Gull Point

Margot Frost, 48, the celebrated crime novelist, has disappeared from the Seagull Hotel, where she had been staying since June to finish her twelfth book. Staff raised the alarm yesterday morning when Miss Frost did not appear for breakfast — something witnesses say had never once happened in eleven weeks.

Her bed had not been slept in. The window of Room 9 stood open. Most puzzling of all, her famous green typewriter — which she carried everywhere and called “the only man I’ve ever trusted” — was gone, along with every page of the unfinished novel, believed to be titled The Silver Tide.

The Seagull Hotel, photographed last summer. Room 9 is beneath the gable.

A search of the cliffs revealed Miss Frost’s grey cardigan folded neatly on the rocks at Gull Point, below which the currents are known to be treacherous. Police say they are “gravely concerned”, and that the sea does not often give back what it takes.

Hotel manager Mr Gerald Pinch, 51, described himself as “devastated beyond words”. “She was our brightest star,” he told the Gazette. “The Seagull was her home. I was her dearest friend on this earth.”

The last person known to have seen Miss Frost is a chambermaid, Rosie Marsh, 17, who took the author her cocoa at nine o’clock on Saturday night. Miss Marsh declined to speak to reporters, and was seen leaving the hotel in tears.

Miss Frost’s black cat, Bram, who sat on her lap each evening in the residents’ lounge, has not been found.

DOCUMENT 4 — THE DIARY OF ROSIE MARSH (1)
Tuesday 30th June, 1987

First week at the Seagull. My feet ache like anything but I don’t care, because guess who is staying in Room 9. MARGOT FROST. The actual real Margot Frost. Mum has read every one of her books from the library, twice. I carried her cocoa up tonight and she looked at me over her glasses and said, “You read on your breaks. I’ve seen you. Which one of mine is your favourite?” and I said the one where the postman did it, and she laughed like a drain and said “Everyone suspects the postman. Nobody suspects the post.” I don’t know what that means but I wrote it down because it sounds important.

Saturday 11th July, 1987

Margot (she says I must call her Margot, “Miss Frost sounds like a fridge”) lets me sit with her when my shift ends. She’s teaching me how mysteries work. She says every mystery has three things: a secret, a lie, and a person brave enough to notice the difference. Her cat Bram sat on my lap the whole time. He only likes three people in the world, she says. Her, me, and absolutely nobody else.

Friday 24th July, 1987

Something horrible today. I was polishing the landing and I heard Mr Pinch in Room 9, sort of hissing. He said, “You owe me, Margot. Half that book is mine and you know it.” And Margot said, cold as anything, “No, Gerald. You locked me in this hotel with a contract, not a kindness. You’ll get what the paper says and not a penny of my pages.” Then the door opened and I had to pretend to polish the same spot of banister for about a year.

Sunday 9th August, 1987

Margot asked me strange questions today. Whether I could keep a secret. Whether I thought a person could be two people in one lifetime. Then she said the oddest thing. She said, “Rosie, when you read a mystery, everyone watches the detective. Nobody watches the luggage.” And she looked at her big trunk in the corner and winked.

DOCUMENT 4 — THE DIARY OF ROSIE MARSH (2)
Saturday 22nd August, 1987 — very late

I am writing this fast in the linen cupboard because I don’t want to forget one single word. Tonight when I brought the cocoa, Margot had the box out on her bed. This box. She put my hand on the lid and said: “Keep this safe and keep it secret. Don’t open it, don’t burn it, don’t give it to anyone — especially not to Gerald Pinch. One day someone in your family will need it. You’ll know them because they’ll find it all by themselves.” Then she said, “Trust the postcards, Rosie.” I said what postcards? and she just smiled and gave Bram a whole sardine.

Monday 24th August, 1987

She’s gone. Everyone is saying she walked into the sea off Gull Point because her cardigan was on the rocks. The police came. I tried to tell the sergeant that it CAN’T be that, and he patted my head — actually patted it, like I was a spaniel — and said “leave the thinking to us, pet.” So I will write my thinking here instead:

ONE. Margot hated swimming. She called the sea “a lot of wet weather with fish in it.”

TWO. Her typewriter is gone. A typewriter weighs as much as a Christmas turkey. You do not carry your typewriter off a cliff.

THREE. Bram is gone too. Cats do not walk into the sea. Nobody takes their cat to a drowning.

FOUR. The cardigan was folded NEATLY. Margot never folded anything neatly in her life. Room 9 looked like a jumble sale every single day.

FIVE. Her big trunk went down to the station on Friday with the luggage van. Nobody watches the luggage.

She is not drowned. She has ESCAPED. And I am the only person in the whole world who knows, and I am seventeen, and nobody will ever believe me.

Room 9, The Seagull Hotel
22nd August 1987

My dear Rosie,

If you are reading this properly — by which I mean years from now, with your heart thumping — then whoever found my box has done exactly what I hoped, and you may tell them from me: well done, detective.

By morning I shall be somebody else. Do not grieve, and do not believe the sea took me. The sea and I have an understanding: it doesn’t write novels and I don’t go paddling.

In this box are my eleven finished manuscripts — my life’s murders, all of them harmless — and my diary of this last strange summer. They are the proof of how I write: my plans, my crossings-out, my tricks. A story’s fingerprints, if you like. Should a certain gentleman ever pretend my words are his, this box will say otherwise, louder than I ever could.

Every summer, no matter where I am, I shall send you a postcard. No message — messages can be read — only a little drawing of Bram. While the postcards come, you will know we are well. The postmark will tell you where to find me, when finding me matters.

Be brave, dear girl. You were the only one who noticed things at that hotel. Noticing is the whole of it.

Your friend,
M.

DOCUMENT 14 — WRITTEN STATEMENT OF “MISS PEGGY MARSH” OF DRIFTWOOD COTTAGE

My name — my first name, the one I was born with and shall no doubt die with — is Margot Frost. I am eighty-seven years old, and I have been dead for thirty-nine of them. I recommend it. The queue at the post office treats you beautifully.

In 1987 I was the most famous mystery writer in England and the unhappiest woman in it. Gerald Pinch had trapped me with a wicked contract signed in a foolish moment, and he took half of everything I earned, and reminded me daily that he always would. I could not out-argue him. I could not out-lawyer him. But — and here I was fortunate — I could out-PLOT him. Plotting was my entire profession.

So I wrote my own vanishing. The folded cardigan (a clumsy clue, folded far too neatly — I hoped somebody would notice, and one dear girl did). The trunk sent ahead from the station. The typewriter in a hat box. Bram in a fish basket, bribed with sardines beyond counting. By Sunday morning Margot Frost was a tragedy, and Peggy Marsh — I borrowed the name of the only trustworthy person I knew — was a plain woman on a bus with a heavy amount of luggage. Nobody watches the luggage.

Then Gerald published my stolen notes as his own, exactly as I feared he might, and I could say nothing — for the dead make poor witnesses. That is why the box exists. Eleven manuscripts and a diary: my fingerprints, waiting in a loft for someone with sharp eyes and no fear of dust.

It seems the wait is over. Rosie — you kept your promise for thirty-nine years, which is thirty-eight more than most people manage. And your granddaughters found it all by themselves, exactly as foretold. The little one, I am told, suspected the cat evidence at once. She will go far.

Signed, in my own unmistakable hand,
MARGOT FROST
(dictated to no one, typed by nobody, entirely alive)
